What actually clutters a LinkedIn feed in 2026 — it's not just ads anymore

Ads get the blame because they're labeled. The rest of what's crowding your feed doesn't announce itself, which is exactly why it adds up to more than the ads do.

Zenfeed — built by one person · Live on the Chrome Web Store

Ask someone why their LinkedIn feed feels cluttered and the answer is almost always the same: too many ads. That's a fair complaint, but by 2026 it's only part of the picture. Promoted posts are labeled, so they're easy to notice and easy to blame. The rest of what's competing for space in your feed isn't labeled at all, which is exactly why most people underestimate how much of it there is.

I spend a lot of time looking at this closely, because it's what Zenfeed is built to filter. Here's what I keep seeing, beyond the obvious ads.

What's actually taking up space in a LinkedIn feed?

Four things share the feed with real posts: promoted posts, suggested content from LinkedIn's own recommendation engine, AI-generated posts, and updates from company and Showcase Pages. Those are the categories I look for when I'm building and testing the extension, and they show up in almost every scroll.

Promoted posts

Sponsored content from companies and creators paying to be in front of you. At least it's labeled, so you can spot it and decide for yourself.

Suggested content

"People you may know," "Suggested for you," and similar prompts from LinkedIn's own recommendation engine — not from anyone you actually follow.

AI-generated posts

Posts written or heavily assisted by AI. The problem isn't that they're worse than a human post — it's that there are so many of them that they bury the posts from people you actually follow.

Company and Showcase Page posts

Updates from company and school pages, mixed into the same feed as your connections, often with no visual signal that separates them from a person's post.

Why ads get most of the blame

Promoted posts carry a small "Promoted" label, so your eye catches them and your brain files the annoyance under "ads." The other three categories don't get that label. A suggested-content card and a real post from someone you follow are built out of the same layout, the same avatar, the same line of text under a name. Nothing tells you, at a glance, which one it is.

That's not an accident of design so much as a side effect of how the feed is built. It isn't sorting posts into "things you asked for" and "things you didn't." It's sorting for what's likely to keep you scrolling, and a suggested connection or an AI-written post about "5 lessons from failure" can do that job just as well as a real update from someone you actually know.

The feed doesn't sort noise from signal for you. It sorts for what keeps you scrolling, and noise does that job just as well as signal does.

A 2-minute way to check your own feed

This isn't a scientific study, just a manual check anyone can run in a couple of minutes, and it's more informative than it sounds. Open LinkedIn on desktop, and without clicking anything, scroll through the first 20 to 25 posts. For each one, mentally tag it: a real post from someone you follow, promoted, suggested, AI-flavored, or a company page post. Then count.

When I've done this on my own feed and heard from other people who've tried it, the real-post count is almost always lower than expected — often under half the posts in that first scroll. The gap isn't made up mostly of ads. It's the other three categories, stacked on top of each other, each one small enough on its own to overlook.

The whole check happens in your own browser, on your own feed. Nothing about it involves sending your feed anywhere or installing anything — it's just a way to actually see what you're scrolling past instead of assuming.

Does unfollowing people fix a cluttered LinkedIn feed?

Unfollowing fixes one of the four categories, not all four. It removes people, not suggested-content cards or company pages or AI posts from accounts you do follow. Muting works case by case, but you'd be muting all day to keep up with a feed this mixed.

That gap between "I can see what's cluttering my feed" and "I can actually do something about all four categories at once" is the reason I built Zenfeed in the first place. On LinkedIn and X, it hides promoted posts, suggested content, AI posts, and — on LinkedIn — company and Showcase Page posts, each with its own on/off toggle, so you're not eyeballing every single post while you scroll. On YouTube it does less, on purpose, and only once you opt in: just Rest from AI, custom keyword blocking, and an opt-in Shorts-shelf toggle on Home, Subscriptions, and search results.

The AI toggle works a little differently from the others: it's a countdown you start, not a switch you leave on. That was a deliberate call rather than something I never got around to, and I wrote up why Rest from AI is a timer rather than a permanent block if you want the reasoning behind it.

Worth saying plainly None of this is about judging AI posts, suggested content, or company pages as bad. It's about volume — there's enough of all four categories, stacked together, that the posts from people you actually follow get buried underneath them.

The 2-minute audit isn't rigorous, and I'd rather say that upfront than dress it up as data. But it's honest, it's free, and it takes less time than reading this post did. If you run it on your own feed and the split looks different from what I've described here, I'd genuinely like to hear about it — that's the kind of thing that changes how I build this.

If you use LinkedIn, X, or YouTube and this problem sounds familiar, feedback is always welcome — what's still slipping through, what's being hidden that shouldn't be, any of it.
